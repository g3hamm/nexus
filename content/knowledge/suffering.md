# Why does God allow suffering?

## What is usually being asked

Almost nobody asks this abstractly. Someone raising it has usually buried a
parent, lost a child, survived something, or watched a war on their phone. The
philosophical question and the human one wear the same words, and answering the
wrong one is the most common mistake a volunteer makes here.

The first move is to find out which is being asked. Often the answer is
neither: they are not asking a question at all, they are saying that something
unbearable happened and they want to know whether God noticed.

## What Christians have historically said

Christian responses have never been a single argument.

**Freedom.** Love that cannot be refused is not love. A world containing people
capable of genuine good is a world containing people capable of genuine harm.
This addresses evil that people do; it says nothing about earthquakes.

**A world in ruins.** Scripture describes creation as damaged and awaiting
repair, not as operating exactly as intended. Suffering is treated as an enemy
throughout — something God is against, not something he is arranging.

**God's own suffering.** The distinctively Christian claim is not that
suffering has a tidy explanation but that God entered it. The cross means
whatever else is true, God is not aloof from pain. This is usually the only
thing worth saying to someone in fresh grief.

**A refusal to explain.** The book of Job spends thirty-seven chapters on
friends explaining Job's suffering, and God's response condemns the explainers,
not the man who complained. Scripture models protest more than it models
justification.

## What not to say

- "Everything happens for a reason." Scripture does not say this, and to
  someone grieving it sounds like their loss was instrumental.
- "God needed another angel." False, and cruel.
- "God won't give you more than you can handle." A misreading of 1 Corinthians
  10:13, which is about temptation, not suffering. Plenty of people are given
  more than they can handle; that is much of what the Psalms are about.
- Any answer offered before the person has finished speaking.

## Where to go instead

Lament is a biblical category and a permitted one. Roughly a third of the
Psalms are complaints, several with no resolution. Psalm 88 ends in darkness.
Pointing someone to the fact that scripture contains prayers as angry as theirs
is often more use than any argument — it tells them their reaction is not
faithlessness.

Honest uncertainty is also permitted. "I don't know why this happened to you,
and I don't think anyone does" is a truthful sentence that a hurting person can
hear.
