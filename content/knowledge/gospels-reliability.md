# Can the gospels be trusted?

## What is usually behind the question

Two quite different concerns share this wording. One is historical: were these
documents written close enough to the events, and transmitted carefully enough,
to tell us anything? The other is suspicion: _this was written by people
selling something._ The second is more common and is rarely answered by
manuscript statistics.

## What can be said with reasonable confidence

**They are early by ancient standards.** Most scholars date the gospels to
roughly 65–100 AD, within living memory of the events. Paul's letters are
earlier still, and 1 Corinthians 15 preserves a summary that most historians —
including sceptical ones — date to within a few years of the crucifixion.

**The manuscript tradition is unusually rich.** Thousands of Greek manuscripts
survive, far more than for any comparable ancient text. This does not prove the
contents true; it means we can reconstruct what was originally written with
good confidence. Those are different claims and conflating them damages your
credibility.

**Textual variants are known and mostly minor.** Modern translations footnote
the significant ones — the ending of Mark, the woman caught in adultery. That
these are printed in the footnotes of ordinary Bibles is itself worth pointing
out; nothing is being concealed.

**The accounts differ.** Volunteers sometimes try to harmonise every detail.
That is not necessary and often looks evasive. Independent witnesses differing
on detail while agreeing on substance is what genuine testimony looks like;
perfectly matching accounts would suggest collusion.

## Where honesty is required

- We do not have the original manuscripts of anything from the ancient world.
- Authorship of several books is genuinely disputed among scholars.
- "Historically reliable" is not the same as "divinely inspired". The first is
  a historical judgement; the second is a theological one that evidence alone
  will not settle.

## A more useful direction

Someone suspicious of the motives behind the documents is often better served
by the awkwardness inside them: the first witnesses to the resurrection are
women, whose testimony carried little legal weight at the time; the disciples
are depicted as cowardly and slow; Jesus says things the early church plainly
found difficult. Invented propaganda usually flatters its authors more than
this.
