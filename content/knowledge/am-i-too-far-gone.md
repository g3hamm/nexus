# "I've done too much to be forgiven"

## Recognise what this is

This is not usually a theological question, and answering it theologically
first is a mistake. Someone saying it is normally telling you something
specific they have done, without saying what it is, and watching to see how you
react.

The most useful first response is not an argument. It is not flinching.

## What not to do

- Do not ask what they did. If they want to say, they will. Asking can read as
  either prurience or as gathering evidence for a judgement.
- Do not rush to reassure. "Of course God forgives you!" delivered in two
  seconds tells them you did not take seriously what they were about to
  confess, and they will stop.
- Do not minimise. "I'm sure it wasn't that bad" is a small dismissal of
  something that may have cost someone a great deal.
- Do not move immediately to a decision or a prayer. They are testing whether
  this is safe.

## What Christians hold

Forgiveness in Christian teaching is not proportional to the offence. That is
the point of it — it is not a scale that can be overwhelmed, because it does
not run on merit. The consistent witness of scripture is of people with serious
histories being received: a persecutor becomes an apostle, a betrayer is
restored, a criminal is promised paradise hours before dying.

Christians have differed on much. That God receives the person who turns toward
him, whatever is behind them, is not one of the contested points.

## Saying it well

Short, plain, unhurried. Something like: _there isn't a category of person this
doesn't reach. Not one._

Then stop talking and let them respond.

## When it is more than guilt

Sometimes this sentence is carrying something heavier — self-harm, or a belief
that they deserve to die. If anything suggests that, care for the person first.
Ask directly whether they are safe. Stay with them. Encourage local emergency
help. Nothing about apologetics matters more than that, and a conversation
about forgiveness can wait until you know they are alive tomorrow.
